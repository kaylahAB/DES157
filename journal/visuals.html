Visual Thinking

October 27, 2026

Reading the New York Times piece on 10 Intriguing Photographs and Visual Thinking Strategies made me realize how rarely I don't make images the focal point but just as filler or a prop for the topic in my personal studies and design works. I usually scan for composition, palette, or "does this sell what I'm trying to convey?" instead of asking: what's going on? What do I see that makes me say that? What more can I find? Trying that with the photos was surprisingly useful. The more I looked, the more I noticed, and I can see the same habit helping me read UI screenshots, concept art, or even how interfaces introduces themselves to a viewer.

For my example, I chose Nomadic Tribe by Makemepulse, and it left me agog. The screen and hover states react to the viewer constantly, and after the introduction screen there is a real sense of immersion. I could drag flowers up from the ground and watch them bloom in the direction I pulled, and the birds flew faster when I hold left click. Those moments answer "what more can I find?" through touch instead of just sight, which is a kind of visual thinking I hadn't considered before. What impressed me most is that the site doesn't need many mechanics or complicated design. A few standout elements and a very strong core concept carry the whole beautiful interactive story.

The drawback is that this richness has a cost. They would expect more of hunt for more interactions or special features and take focus away form the story, or the possibilities of people having slower connections, or small screens. I'd love to see an auto play mode that lets everyone slow down and look.

Expanding Our Thinking

October 29, 2026

Part One: Looking at Another Student’s Image

Doing this exchange made me realize how I don't practice seeing an image in an opposing perspective. Rebecca, the partner that was analyzing my images, looked at my astronaut helmet that I designed and immediately talked about solitude and lonleyness, but she also said the pixel style made it feel almost fun and adventurous. While it was hald true the image is supposed to spark a feeling of discovery and the unknown, lonleyness was a contrast to my groups intent. I thought the dark visor and empty space were just setting a mood of a more serious tone for a more mature setting for a pixel game. She treated them as the main carriers of feeling, and the playful medium as something that complicated the mood instead of canceling it out. Having both tones in the same frame actually made the image stronger than if it had stayed purely serious or purely game-like.

What surprised me most was how much the picture was already saying without much explanation from me. The single figure and the solid reflective visor were doing most of the work. Rebecca picked up the darker emotional register and the sense of quiet searching right away. That made me pay closer attention to which parts of an image carry the real weight, and it also made me think about the interfaces we have been building. Sometimes the screen can communicate before any text or story is added. The most ordinary-looking details can end up being the most open-ended ones.

Part Two: Looking at My Own Image

After reviewing the partner exercise and reading the article, I appriciated my partners interpetation of my image. When designing an illistration or maiking an image I focus on the emotional tone first and let the style follow. What you cannot tell just by looking; I wanted the isolation of the helmet to feel quiet and agog but now it setts the feeling of isolation, that perspective made the image piece to become more heavy, considering the limited pixel language; it kept a sense of play and adventure in the frame even while the subject stayed sparse. That push and pull is what still interests me most about it.

This image sits near the center of the collection I am building around in-between states—moments of being between places or identities. The helmet can be read as protection, as a blank surface, or as something left behind. In that way the whole set of images is less a straight record and more a group of visual questions. If I were to push the image further, I would probably work more with the reflection in the visor, letting something faint and unclear appear there, or cool the colors down so the pixels feel a little more metallic and distant. Either change would move it further from a simple documentary look and deeper into the tension that already lives between the subject and the way it is drawn. Or just change the image entirly, lol.
